  • 3 A page name that has nothing to do with dentistry These ads often run from generic pages whose names sound like a news alert, a wellness blog or a consumer bulletin rather than a dental practice. A research site does not hide behind a nondescript brand.
  • 4 "See If You Qualify" with no medical screening Genuine eligibility screening asks clinical questions and ends with a licensed clinician examining you. A one click qualification form that only wants your name, phone and email is collecting contact data, not screening patients.
  • 5 Money moving toward them, in any form A registration fee, an application fee, a processing fee, a deposit, or a request for your bank or card details is disqualifying on its own. The FTC guidance is explicit: never pay to be part of a clinical trial, or to find out about one.
  • 6 Gift cards, countdowns and "last call" urgency Gift card incentives, expiring spots and pressure to claim your place today are sales tactics. Research recruitment does not expire this afternoon, and approval is never instant.
  • 7 Requests for your Social Security number The FTC states that a legitimate trial should never ask for your Social Security number during recruitment or screening, and should not need your bank account or routing number. Real studies that compensate participants can pay by check.

A real clinical trial versus the ad you saw

How to check a clinical trial in about two minutes

You do not have to take our word for any of this. Federal consumer guidance gives you a short, concrete checklist, and the registry is public and free to search.

Look the study up on ClinicalTrials.gov

This is the official registry maintained by the National Institutes of Health and the National Library of Medicine. Search the study or the sponsor. If a trial that claims to be recruiting is not listed there, treat that as your answer.

Find the university, hospital or government sponsor

Real dental implant research runs out of dental schools, academic medical centers and teaching hospitals. The FTC puts it plainly: if no government agency, university or hospital is mentioned, it is likely a scam.

Check who would actually treat you

You should be able to find a named, licensed clinician and a real address before you share anything. In Florida you can verify any dentist through the Department of Health license verification portal.

Apply the payment test

Never pay to enroll, and never hand over your Social Security number, bank account or routing number during screening. If money is moving toward the advertiser, the conversation is over.

Primary sources: NIDCR (NIH) on finding dental care , ClinicalTrials.gov , the Federal Trade Commission on telling a real clinical trial from a fake one and on whether social media ads are vetted . Suspected fraud can be reported at reportfraud.ftc.gov .

You gave them your information. Do this now.

This is the part almost nobody writes about, and it is the most urgent. Nothing here makes you careless. These ads are engineered to be convincing.

  • Stop replying, and do not send a deposit, a fee or any payment.
  • Do not share your Social Security number, bank account, routing number, or a photo of your ID or card.
  • If you already sent card or bank details, call your bank or card issuer now and ask them to flag or freeze the account.
  • Consider placing a free fraud alert with the credit bureaus if you shared identifying information.
  • Report the ad inside the platform it ran on, and report the offer to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov.
  • Expect follow up calls. Contact information collected this way is frequently resold, so more offers may arrive from other numbers.
