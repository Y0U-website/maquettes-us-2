What Happens During Your First Visit

Walk in, and here’s what happens. No mystery, no surprise forms shoved in your face five minutes before you’re seen.

First, we take digital X-rays. These use less radiation than old film, and you’ll see the images pop up on a screen right in front of you. We walk through what we’re looking at together. Cavities, bone levels, anything hiding below the gumline, it’s all right there, not hidden in some folder you never get to see.

Next comes the exam itself. The doctor checks your teeth, your gums, your bite, and does an oral cancer screening too. If something looks off, you’ll hear about it in plain words, not dental jargon that leaves you nodding along without understanding a thing.

For some patients, especially anyone dealing with jaw pain, missing teeth, or a complicated dental history, we’ll bring in the cone beam CT scanner. This gives a full 3D view of your mouth, jaw, and nerves. It used to mean a separate referral. Here, it’s part of the same visit, shown to you on-screen, same as the X-rays.

Then we talk. What we found, what it means, and what your options are.

No pressure to decide anything on the spot.

A typical new patient exam runs about an hour, sometimes a little less. You’ll leave with a clear picture of where your teeth stand today, not a vague everything’s fine and a bill. We’d rather you understand your own mouth than just trust us blindly. The screen does most of the talking.

How New Patient Exam Pricing Works

Let’s talk money for a second, because it’s on your mind. New patient exam costs can look different from one dental office to the next, and that confuses people. Here’s why.

A new patient exam usually bundles a few things together. You’ve got the actual exam, digital X-rays, and sometimes a screening for oral cancer. Some offices charge for each piece separately. Others bundle it into one flat visit. That bundling is what drives the price you see quoted anywhere.

What pushes a price up or down? A few things. Whether X-rays are included matters a lot, since X-rays alone can cost more than the exam itself at some places. Whether a cone beam CT scan is needed adds another layer. That’s a bigger, 3D image used mostly for implant planning or tricky root canal cases. Not every new patient needs one. Insurance status changes things too, since a plan may cover part of the visit or none of it depending on your coverage.

Before you book anywhere, ask a few questions. Does the exam include X-rays, or is that extra? Is there a separate fee if a cone beam scan gets recommended? Will someone walk you through what they find, or just hand you a bill?

We think that last question matters most. Most patients don’t push back on a treatment plan, they just want to understand it. That’s the whole point of showing you your own X-rays and scans on a screen instead of just telling you what we saw. You should see the evidence before you decide anything.

So don’t shop on price alone. An exam that skips X-rays isn’t a better deal, it just moves the cost to your next visit.

When You Don’t Need a New Patient Exam

Let’s be honest for a second. Not everyone walking around Rexburg needs a new patient exam right now, and we’d rather tell you that straight than waste your Tuesday evening slot.

If you’re already an established patient here and you’re just due for a routine cleaning, you don’t need this. A new patient exam is built for someone starting fresh, someone whose mouth we haven’t mapped out yet with X-rays and a full look at your gums, bite, and jaw. If we already have your records on file, you’d just book a regular cleaning or checkup instead.

Same goes if you had a full set of X-rays taken somewhere else in the last six months or so and can bring those records with you. Ask your old office to send them over. That can save you a repeat X-ray series, and we’ll tell you straight if we still need new images for a clear picture.

And if you’re in real pain right now, swelling, a broken tooth, something that can’t wait, that’s not a new patient exam situation. That’s an emergency visit. Different appointment, different urgency.

One more case worth mentioning. Missionaries heading out soon have their own exam built for their timeline and needs, not the standard new patient visit.

So before you book, ask yourself: do I have recent X-rays somewhere else? Am I already a patient here? Is this urgent pain? Any yes changes what you should schedule. We’d rather point you to the right appointment than fill a slot that doesn’t fit you.
